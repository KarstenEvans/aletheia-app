Pip and the Maple's Secret

Free companion notes for curious children, families and adults who still enjoy a good mystery. Optional books and gifts are separate from the story itself.

Reading aloud together

This story has three trails: the child's question-shaped key, Bramble's questionable map, and Pip's missing drawing. Invite children to notice each one and predict how they connect.

Try a Bramble voice

Give Bramble great confidence and absolutely terrible directions. Pause before the child points out a mistake.

Look for clues

After Scene 2 ask: why would a leaf and a map show matching halves of a butterfly wing?

Make the resolution matter

Revisit the plaque and the map in the final scene. The answer should feel earned, not magically inserted.

Quiet mode: read the text at your own pace without narration, visual motion or sound. Dramatic reading never requires frightening a child.

Books worth exploring

These are editorial reading suggestions, not claims that particular editions are currently stocked. Different ages enjoy different kinds of stories. Browse freely here before visiting any retailer.

The Gruffalo

Julia Donaldson and Axel Scheffler. For a younger read-aloud audience: rhythmic storytelling, clever reversals and controlled suspense. A useful lesson in performance.

Matilda

Roald Dahl. A child who questions unfair adult authority, with imagination and mischievous humour. Better for confident independent readers or shared reading.

The Secret Garden

Frances Hodgson Burnett. Hidden places, personal change and a garden full of discovery; its historical language and attitudes benefit from adult discussion.

The Wild Robot

Peter Brown. A robot learning among woodland animals, opening useful questions about technology, compassion, identity and nature.

Books, authors, editions, prices and suitability should be checked by the reader. The summaries here are Aletheia's original editorial descriptions, not extracts from books.

Optional Books & Gifts

Ideas that make sense with Pip: a pocket notebook for clues, a real compass for mapmaking, a magnifying glass for leaves and insects, or a sketchbook to create a butterfly drawing. None is necessary to enjoy the story.

Forest facts to explore

Talking trees and memory-coloured leaves are fictional. Real trees, bees and butterflies have plenty of wonderful science of their own.

  • Woodland Trust: trees, forests and practical ways to identify woodland features.
  • Bumblebee Conservation Trust: how bumblebees live and why habitats matter.
  • Butterfly Conservation: butterfly and moth identification and conservation.
