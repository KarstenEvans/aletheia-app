## Evidence
1. E. Concepción, P. Gervás, G. Méndez (2020), "Exploring Baselines for Combining Full Plots into Multiple-plot Stories", New Generation Computing, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00354-020-00115-x — multiple character plots can be woven by related events, timing, co-occurrence and shared resolution. Computational story modelling, **not** proof of a universal formula for popularity.
2. H. Purser, J. Van Herwegen, M. Thomas (2020), "The development of children's comprehension and appreciation of riddles", Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022096519301432 — among children aged 4–11, understanding and enjoyment differ; smiles/laughter are not automatic proof that a child understood a riddle.
3. T. Shultz (1972), "The role of incongruity and resolution in children's appreciation of cartoon humor", https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0022096572900744 — investigates unexpectedness plus comprehensible resolution.
4. Shultz & Horibe (1974), "Development of the Appreciation of Verbal Jokes", https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ093370 — patterns shift in ages 6–12; older readers often appreciate structural resolution as well as surprise.
5. Rachel Neumeier (2025), "Braided Narratives", https://www.rachelneumeier.com/2025/12/02/braided-narratives/ — author's craft description of braided plots and joint climax; practical perspective rather than controlled evidence.
6. Penguin Random House publisher bibliographic page, https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/353841/blott-on-the-landscape-by-sharpe-tom/9781446474556 — *Blott on the Landscape* by Tom Sharpe, farce with conflict and escalating bureaucratic misunderstandings. Adult material is **not** copied into children's adaptation.

## Editorial analysis and direction
Original Copilot source has a delightful visual image, but its recurring unthreatening wonders do not generate enough causal uncertainty for independent school-age readers. Preserve its six scenes, names, gentle emotional ending, and owner/creator provenance. Add clear consequence and agency instead of frightening peril, teasing or random punchlines.

Three braided trails: (A) CHILD asks why their question-key won't fit an ordinary lock, searches for a promise; (B) BRAMBLE, proud cartographer who confuses directions and symbols, finds the map ink/leaf symbols don't match; (C) PIP is searching for the drawing because it is a signal from another child. Each clue must perform an actual causal job at the finale. Show concrete places and constraints; resolve how the child and map reveal the tucked-away paper. Last scene should pay off an early joke and a clue, not merely repeat “wonder”.

Humour: let Bramble announce impossible certifications, mistake a map orientation and later use that same mistake to notice reverse-written instruction; vary escalation (setup → wrong assumption → correction) and optional wordplay (“What's black and white and read all over? A newspaper”, if used as a credited common riddle, do not claim invention). Avoid relying on pain/fear/gross-out, and avoid equating ADHD with immaturity. Short chapter beats and generous pauses; not overstuffed six equal subplots. One story + three interdependent trails.
