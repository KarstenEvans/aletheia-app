Schrödinger and the Pesky Shield Bugs

When I was very small, I was not technically a cat.

I looked like a cat.

That was different.

I was a Dragonfold, which meant I came from a family of dragons who had discovered that being enormous was terribly inconvenient indoors.

Doors were too small.
Sofas burst.
Teacups became thimbles.
And if you sneezed at breakfast, somebody usually had to rebuild the chimney.

So Dragonfolds learned to fold.

Wings folded.
Tails folded.
Thunder folded.
Several acres of dragon folded.

And eventually, if you did it properly, you were left with something about the size of a kitten.

I was very good at folding.

My mother said I was suspiciously good at it.

“Schrödinger,” she told me one morning, “there are three rules for young Dragonfolds.”

I sat very neatly.

“Rule one: do not unfold in the kitchen.”

I nodded.

“Rule two: do not swallow anything that is glowing unless you know why it is glowing.”

I nodded again.

“Rule three: NEVER bother the shield bugs.”

I stopped nodding.

“Why?”

“Because they are revolting.”

That was not enough information.

Adults often make this mistake.

They think the word revolting will make a child walk away.

It does not.

It makes the child wonder exactly HOW revolting.

That afternoon, a silver saucer landed behind our moon-rock shed.

Out bounced three Flurkins.

Moon Flurkins are rather like cats, except they bounce higher, their whiskers glow, and they consider gravity to be an optional suggestion.

“Game?” shouted one.

“Game!” I shouted back.

We played Crater Chase.

We played Tail Tag.

We played Who Can Bounce Over the Biggest Rock Without Landing in Bloop’s Lunch.

Bloop was not there.

We used somebody else’s lunch.

Then we played Stringball.

This is the most important game in the known universe.

There is a string.

There is a ball.

Nobody remembers the rules after that.

I pounced.

A Flurkin pounced.

Another Flurkin pounced on the first Flurkin.

Someone bounced backwards into a dust pile.

I accidentally unfolded one wing.

“CHEATING!” cried Captain Mizzle.

“Natural ability,” I said.

Then we saw it.

A tiny green creature sat on a moon leaf.

It was shiny.
It was squat.
It was shaped like a little shield.

All four of us froze.

I remembered my mother.

NEVER bother the shield bugs.

I remembered the word revolting.

Then I remembered that adults can be unnecessarily dramatic.

I crept closer.

The bug did nothing.

Closer.

Still nothing.

Closer.

Its little green shield lifted.

The Flurkins took three enormous bounces backwards.

I stayed exactly where I was.

This was partly bravery.

It was mostly because one paw was stuck under the stringball.

The shield bug gave a tiny wiggle.

PFFFFT!

Something pale and sour-smelling squirted through the air.

It missed my nose by the width of a whisker.

The smell arrived anyway.

Imagine an onion.

Now imagine that onion has been left in a sock.

Now imagine the sock belongs to a troll who has just completed a sponsored swamp walk.

It was worse than that.

The Flurkins screamed.

One bounced into a crater.

One bounced out of the crater.

Captain Mizzle bounced so far away that his glowing whiskers became punctuation.

I sat perfectly still.

The shield bug stared at me.

I stared at the shield bug.

There are moments in life when two ancient species understand each other completely.

This was not one of them.

The bug did another little wiggle.

I ran.

I ran past the saucer.

I ran around the moon-rock shed.

I ran through the Stringball finals.

I ran straight into my mother.

She looked down at me.

Then she smelled me.

Her eyes narrowed.

“Shield bug?”

“Possibly.”

“Rule three?”

“Under review.”

Mother sighed the sigh of a dragon who had folded several acres of herself into a patient parent and was beginning to regret the efficiency of the process.

Then she laughed.

That was worse.

The Flurkins came bouncing back.

Captain Mizzle landed beside us wearing his cape over his nose.

“We have invented a new game,” he announced.

“What is it?”

“NOT TOUCHING THE SHIELD BUG.”

It became extremely popular.

Years later, an orange Earth cat called Rocket would bounce across those same moon craters with the Flurkins.

And much later, on Earth, a curious kitten called Ike would meet a green shield bug of her own.

Ike did not run away.

Ike did not merely investigate.

Ike swallowed the bug.

Whole.

When I heard the story, I thought about my mother’s third rule.

I thought about the smell.

I thought about the Flurkins disappearing over the horizon.

Then I thought about Ike licking her lips.

“Fantastic,” she had apparently decided.

Dragonfolds are difficult to surprise.

Ike managed it.

And somewhere, I am quite certain, a green shield bug told its children:

“There are three rules.

Rule one: avoid kittens.

Rule two: definitely avoid Flurkins.

Rule three: if the kitten is called Ike...

RUN.”
