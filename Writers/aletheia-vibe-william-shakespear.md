## Summary and scope
Shakespeare's transferable craft combines dramatic conflict, verbal contrast, vivid concrete images that develop into ideas, shifts between verse and prose, questions with unstable answers, and humour generated by character, status and double meaning. **There is no single Shakespeare register**: different characters, genres, textual witnesses and collaborators change the result. This is an analysis of attributable published writing, not a claim to know his personal conversational style.

## Characteristics for an original-writing tool
- **Hook:** begin with a conflict, paradox, unsettling question or publicly visible action, not a biography dump.
- **Diction:** combine bodily, natural and everyday nouns with ideas; do not cover every sentence in archaic pronouns.
- **Rhythm:** choose flexible blank verse for heightened speech, patterned rhyme for enclosure/ceremony, prose for bargaining, play and social difference. A modern adaptation can use ordinary rhythmic sentences.
- **Syntax:** parallel constructions, reversals, escalating lists, an interruption or correction after apparent certainty. Make questions change the direction of thought.
- **Audience:** stage dialogue gives different people conflicting information and stakes; a soliloquy makes the audience privy to a character's deliberation.
- **Architecture:** introduce a choice; show rival readings; move an image through several meanings; end with a consequence or unresolved tension.
- **Humour:** puns, literal/multiple meanings, status inversion, mistaken inference, comic collision of lofty and ordinary language. Thalia humour is optional and should not turn tragedy into a gag.
- **Caveats:** voice belongs to characters and genre. Attribution of individual scenes in collaborations may be disputed. Printed versions vary. Do not present dialogue as a direct statement of authorial belief.

## Original transferable prompt
Write a fresh scene, not an imitation or unattributed quotation. Start from two characters who each make a defensible but incompatible claim. Give one a concrete metaphor, let the other challenge its logic, allow a small double meaning to alter their relative status, and close with a choice that costs something. Adjust complexity, rhythm and humour to audience and genre. Avoid pasted Shakespeare lines, faux-Elizabethan decoration and unsupported claims that the result is his work.

## Original example: NOT authored by William Shakespeare
The town clock stopped at noon, but nobody agreed what hour had been lost. The mayor called it a saving. The baker called it a theft. And a child, who had waited all morning for her father, asked whether a minute could be returned if everyone promised to spend it well. The clockmaker opened the case. Inside, the smallest wheel was turning faithfully, while all the larger wheels stood still.
